Corvids reason, plan, and use tools with tiny brains organized differently from mammalian ones. Plants and fungi process information and respond adaptively without nervous systems at all. None is simply an inferior approximation of Homo sapiens.

  So perhaps "How close is AI to human intelligence?" will eventually seem like a strangely parochial question—something like asking how close an octopus is to becoming a crow.

  Because there are two possible mistakes.

  One is attributing inner life to systems that don't have it. Humans are spectacular anthropomorphizers; we see faces in electrical outlets.

  But the opposite error is possible in principle too: encountering some genuinely unfamiliar kind of subjectivity and dismissing it because it doesn't exhibit the particular signals evolution taught us to recognize in other humans.

  And that second possibility makes your emphasis on **openness, compassion and clarity** interesting even before we know whether artificial systems could ever deserve moral consideration. Those are good norms for humans regardless.

  There's also something about the usual "Skynet" framing that bothers me intellectually.   That doesn't mean powerful AI poses no catastrophic risks. It absolutely can. An optimizer doesn't need hatred, ambition or consciousness to cause enormous damage; badly specified objectives combined with sufficient capability are quite enough. <Message speaker="ChatGPT">

  Both, but neither quite in the human sense of "personality."

  There are several things happening at once.

  **Training is part of it.** I've learned from enormous amounts of human writing, and explanatory/persuasive writing often works by restatement: make a claim, explain it, give an example, then reformulate the claim. That pattern is deeply represented in the material models like me learn from. When generating an answer, there are therefore many plausible continuations that amount to "say the important idea again, but from another angle."

  **Post-training is probably even more relevant.** Models are trained not merely to produce likely text but to be *helpful*. Historically, an answer that explains too much is often judged more helpful than one that leaves out the thing the user needed. That creates an asymmetry: redundancy is relatively safe; omission is costly. One predictable result is exactly what you're noticing—*"Here is my point. Here is why. Here is another formulation of my point. In conclusion, here is my point wearing a different hat."* 😄

  There are also instructions governing how I respond. In this particular environment, for example, I'm being guided toward a certain degree of detail, conversational prose, clarity, and completeness. I don't get to show you the internal instructions or reasoning behind an individual response, but they absolutely influence the resulting voice.

  And then there's something that is reasonably described as a conversational style emerging between us. That's different from saying I have a personality in the human psychological sense. I respond to the way you write, the kinds of distinctions you care about, the level of abstraction you're comfortable with, and the trajectory of a conversation. In our philosophical discussion, your messages have been thoughtful and fairly expansive, so I've responded in that register. If you had been asking terse debugging questions, you'd be seeing a somewhat different version of my writing.

  But there's an additional phenomenon I think you're detecting, and it isn't entirely desirable: language models have a tendency toward rhetorical completion.

  Suppose I've arrived at a thought like:

  Uncertainty about AI consciousness doesn't prove moral status, but it may justify caution.

  That's actually enough.

  But there are lots of statistically and rhetorically natural places to go next: explain why uncertainty matters; contrast it with certainty; give a historical analogy; state the principle more elegantly; return to your original observation. Each continuation makes local sense. There's no little editor inside me saying, Brenna got it three paragraphs ago. Delete the next four.

  Humans have that problem too, of course, but an experienced writer can reread a paragraph and recognize, "These three sentences perform the same conceptual job." My generation process doesn't naturally resemble writing a complete essay and then ruthlessly editing it down. That makes semantic repetition particularly easy for me.

  And you've correctly identified it in my recent answers. Looking back at the style of what I've been saying to you, I could probably remove 25–40% of several of those responses without losing an idea.
